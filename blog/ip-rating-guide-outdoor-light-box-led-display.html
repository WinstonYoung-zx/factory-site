IP Ratings for Outdoor Displays and Enclosures: Choosing Between IP54, IP65, IP66 and IP67

2026-11-05 · 8 min read · Engineering Team, ZhanXin

Almost every outdoor specification we receive contains a protection rating. Usually it reads "IP65". Occasionally it reads "IP67" — added by someone who reasoned that a bigger number is safer.

That reasoning is understandable and frequently wrong. A higher second digit does not automatically cover the conditions that lower digits describe, and in outdoor installations the difference between IP65 and IP66 matters far more to service life than the step up to IP67.

This guide explains what the numbers actually mean, where the common specification mistakes are made, and which ratings fit real installation conditions for light boxes, wayfinding structures and outdoor LED displays.

Reading the code

IEC 60529 defines the Ingress Protection (IP) code as two characteristic numerals with optional letters:

IP   5   6   K
     │   │   └─ optional letter (shows the code follows ISO 20653 vehicle standard)
     │   └───── protection against water ingress
     └───────── protection against solid objects and dust

An "X" means that characteristic was not tested — IPX7 tells you nothing at all about dust protection.

First numeral: solids and dust

NumeralProtected againstAccess probe
0No protection—
1Objects 50 mm and largerBack of hand
2Objects 12.5 mm and largerFinger
3Objects 2.5 mm and largerTool
4Objects 1.0 mm and largerWire
5Dust protected — ingress not fully prevented, but no harmful deposit permittedWire
6Dust tight — no ingress of dust permittedWire

For outdoor enclosures holding electronics, ask for 6 rather than 5. The difference is not trivial: a dust-protected enclosure still allows fine dust to enter, and fine dust combines with humidity to form a conductive film on driver boards and LED drivers.

Second numeral: water

NumeralProtected againstTypical test condition
0No protection—
1Vertically falling dropsDrip box, vertical
2Drops at up to 15° from verticalTilting drip box
3Spraying waterOscillating tube or spray nozzle, ±60°
4Splashing water from any directionSplash, limited ingress permitted
5Water jets6.3 mm nozzle, approx. 12.5 L/min, 3 m distance
6Powerful water jets12.5 mm nozzle, approx. 100 L/min, 3 m distance
7Temporary immersion30 minutes at 1 m depth
8Continuous immersionDepth and duration set by manufacturer, beyond IPX7
9High-pressure, high-temperature water jetsClose-range, ~80 °C, high pressure cleaning

Look at the test conditions for 5 and 6. IP6X takes roughly eight times the water flow of IP5X. IP7 subjects the enclosure to still water at a fixed depth — it does not involve a jet at all.

Why IP67 does not replace IP66

This is the single most costly misunderstanding in outdoor specification.

The second numeral does not cascade downward. An enclosure rated IP67 has been tested for temporary immersion; unless it was also tested for powerful jets, it has demonstrated nothing about its ability to withstand driving rain, pressure washing or storm-driven water against a façade. It is entirely possible — and common — for an enclosure to pass IP67 and fail IP66.

So when you see a single rating:

Stated ratingWhat you actually knowWhat you do not know
IP65Withstands water jetsNothing about powerful jets or immersion
IP67Survives 30 min at 1 m depthNothing about jets. Dust performance unknown if written as IPX7
IP65 + IP67Both conditions testedNothing about powerful jets (IP66)
IP66 + IP67Powerful jets and immersionNothing about IK impact, salt spray, or UV aging

For street-level installations, IP66 is usually the rating that matters most. Equipment mounted beside a road, along a platform edge or in a coastal promenade is exposed to wind-driven rain and regular pressure washing — the IP66 condition. Temporary immersion is a reality only for equipment in flood-prone locations, below grade, or in drainage channels.

A practical specification therefore often reads: IP66 + IP67, plus the additional durability clauses below.

Condensation: the failure mode IP ratings do not cover

Here is a problem that catches almost every first-time outdoor buyer.

Outdoor enclosures heat up in the sun and cool rapidly after dark. Inside an illuminated unit, the difference can be significant. Warm air holds moisture; when it cools, that moisture condenses on the inside of the enclosure — on the diffuser, on the LED board, on the driver.

An enclosure can be perfectly sealed to IP67 and still fill with internally generated water, because IP testing addresses water trying to get in, not water already inside trying to get out.

Three responses, in order of preference:

  1. Breathable vent plugs — a hydrophobic membrane that equalises pressure and lets water vapour escape while blocking liquid water. This is the standard answer for outdoor electronics enclosures and should be specified by default for LED units.
  2. Drainage provisions — a low-point weep path, positioned so that it cannot wick water upward. Requires maintenance attention and can compromise other ratings if poorly executed.
  3. Active dehumidification — a small heater or desiccant system. Justified only for high-value, continuously monitored equipment.

Note the conflict this creates with purely "sealed" thinking: a drainage hole and a higher IP rating work against each other. Ask your supplier how the enclosure manages internal humidity, not just what it is rated.

The four things IP ratings do not tell you

IP spray/immersion regimes are unchanged since the mid-20th century. They say nothing about several durability issues that drive premature degradation in outdoor installations. In specification documents they should be listed separately.

RequirementStandard to referenceWhy it matters
Impact resistanceIEC 62262 / EN 62262 (IK code, IK07 to IK10)Public furniture gets struck by carts, luggage and cleaning equipment. An IP rating says nothing about impact resistance. For street-level enclosures specify IK08 or higher.
Salt spray / corrosionISO 9227 (neutral salt spray) or ASTM B117Critical for coastal and de-iced road environments. Ask for tested hours and substrate. This is separate from IP.
Organic coating durabilityQualicoat / GSB / AAMA classes for aluminium; generally ISO 12944 for steel coatingsChalking and fading are the most common visible failures, and IP does not touch them.
Fire performanceEN 13501-1 (EU) or UL 94 (material flammability)Mandatory for indoor and semi-indoor applications; relevant to any diffuser or panel material.

A coastal, high-traffic platform installation therefore needs something closer to: IP66 + IP67, IK08, 1000 h neutral salt spray per ISO 9227, Qualicoat Class 2 coating. Four independent requirements, only one of which is an IP rating.

How this maps to the products we build

Our own units reflect the same logic. A wall-mounted light box intended for a transit corridor specifies an aluminium profile frame with a gasketed door and 6 mm glazing; a larger pillar-wrapped unit with a 100–240 V driver and LED source specifies an outdoor-rated construction and, where it is installed at street level, the higher jet rating rather than relying on an immersion figure that its location does not call for.

Every outdoor enclosure we supply can be delivered with IEC 60529 IP test reports, alongside third-party coating thickness readings and salt spray results for coastal projects. If your specification names a combination such as IP66 + IP67 + IK08, state all three up front — the combination changes gasket specification, venting strategy, and sometimes the frame section, so it is far cheaper to decide during drawing than during production.

Material choices interact with all of this: whether the frame is aluminium profile or galvanized steel affects how the enclosure behaves in a salt spray environment long after the IP certificate was issued. Frequently asked questions

Is IP67 better than IP65?

Not in any meaningful general sense. IP67 tests immersion; IP65 tests water jets. Neither implies the other. For most outdoor installations IP65 or IP66 is the specification that matters, and going beyond what the location requires only adds cost.

Can one enclosure carry two ratings?

Yes, and for demanding installations it should. Enclosures are commonly tested and marked as IP66 + IP67, and increasingly IP69 for wash-down environments. Make sure each declared combination was actually tested — ask for the reports, not just the marking.

Does an IP rating last for the life of the product?

The rating is demonstrated on a new unit under laboratory conditions. Gaskets age, UV exposure degrades polymer parts, and fastener compression relaxes. Ask about gasket material — EPDM and silicone behave quite differently over time — and about the recommended replacement interval.

What is the "K" in IP69K?

It is not part of IEC 60529. IP69K belongs to ISO 20653, the road-vehicle standard, where the K indicates this code and its associated high-pressure hot-water test. Do not use it for general building applications unless asked to.

Do I need anything beyond IP for coastal projects?

Yes. Specify salt spray hours per ISO 9227, coating class, and material selection. Salt-driven corrosion is not an ingress phenomenon.
