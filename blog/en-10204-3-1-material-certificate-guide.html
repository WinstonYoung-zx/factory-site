EN 10204 Material Certificates Explained: 2.1, 2.2, 3.1 and 3.2 Compared

2026-11-05 · 8 min read · Engineering Team, ZhanXin

Few documents are requested as often and understood as little as the EN 10204 material certificate. It appears in nearly every serious fabrication enquiry, usually as the single requirement "material certificates to EN 10204 3.1 must be provided" — and then nobody checks what arrives.

That is a missed opportunity, because this document is cheap, standardised, and genuinely diagnostic. If you know what to look at, it tells you within about a minute whether the supplier operates real traceability or is producing paperwork on demand.

This guide covers what the four types mean, why the most common assumption about type 3.1 is wrong, and how to read one properly.

The four types

EN 10204 is titled Metallic products — Types of inspection documents. It defines the document types, not the material quality. A certificate can be perfectly valid in form and still describe material you would not want.

TypeNameWho validatesContains test results?Based on specific inspection of this batch?
2.1Declaration of compliance with the orderManufacturerNoNo
2.2Test reportManufacturerYes, from non-specific inspectionNo — results are from routine testing, not necessarily this batch
3.1Inspection certificate 3.1Manufacturer's authorised inspection representative, independent of the manufacturing departmentYes, from specific inspectionYes — this batch
3.2Inspection certificate 3.2Manufacturer's representative and the purchaser's representative or a third partyYes, from specific inspectionYes — this batch

The critical distinction sits between "specific" and "non-specific" inspection. Specific inspection means the tests were carried out on the material you are buying, or on samples taken from it. Non-specific means the manufacturer tested something else representative enough to publish numbers.

The misunderstanding that costs buyers money

Type 3.1 is not a third-party certificate.

This is the single most expensive assumption in material documentation. Type 3.1 is issued by the manufacturer itself. The requirement is only that it is validated by someone within the manufacturer's organisation who is authorised to do so and is independent of the production department — an internal separation of duties, nothing more.

If you need a genuinely external witness of the testing, that is type 3.2, which requires validation by the purchaser's representative, a nominated inspection body, or another agreed party.

Consider what this means practically. A type 3.1 certificate:

  • Confirms specific test results for your batch ✓
  • Confirms an independent internal party signed it off ✓
  • Does not involve anyone outside the factory ✓✗

None of this makes 3.1 weak. It is the correct, standard, and sufficient requirement for the vast majority of fabrication work — including the structural supply covered by our EN 1090 execution class guide. It simply is not what many buyers believe it is, and specifying 3.2 when you meant 3.1 adds inspection cost and lead time for a benefit you may not need.

Decide deliberately, using this rough guide:

SituationSensible requirement
Non-load-bearing decorative metalwork2.2 often adequate
Load-bearing fabricated components in ordinary building work3.1 — the standard expectation
Higher consequence classes (EXC3 / EXC4), pressure equipment, or where contractually mandated3.2, witnessed or co-validated

How to read a 3.1 certificate

When the document arrives, work through it in this order. Any serious problem usually appears within the first four checks.

1. Does the heat or cast number appear, and does it match the physical material? This is the most important question and the most often skipped. A certificate describes a batch. If that batch number cannot be matched to the product in your hands — through material marking, tags, or a documented traceability record — then the certificate describes something, just not necessarily what you are buying.

2. Is the material designation complete and to a named standard? "S275JR" to EN 10025 is a specification. "Good quality steel" is not. For aluminium you are looking for a designation such as EN AW-6063 with temper (T5 or T6). If only a trade or generic name appears, ask for the standard-based designation.

3. Is the chemical composition table present with actual values? The C, Mn, Si, P and S figures for steel, or the alloying elements for aluminium, should be listed for your batch. Type 2.2 may show typical or average values; type 3.1 should show the test result for the material supplied.

4. Are the mechanical properties present? Typically yield strength, tensile strength and elongation after fracture. Where impact toughness or through-thickness properties were specified, those values should also appear. Check whether they meet the standard's minima for the stated grade.

5. Is the document signed, dated, and stamped by the authorised representative? A 3.1 without a named signatory and date is not a valid 3.1.

6. Do dimensions, quantity and product form match your order? Certificates often describe the parent batch rather than your finished order. That is acceptable — and normal — provided the traceability in step 1 holds.

What a certificate cannot tell you

Three limits worth knowing before you rely on one:

It certifies the raw material, not your fabricated part. The certificate describes the sheet, plate or extrusion as purchased. Everything done to it after that — welding, forming, coating, machining — is covered by other documents: WPS and PQR records, coating thickness readings, dimensional inspection reports.

It does not prove lack of substitution downstream. This is why material marking transfer matters. If a workshop cuts a certified plate into twenty components and uses no marking or transfer process, those twenty parts are indistinguishable from uncertified stock by the time they reach assembly. Ask how traceability is maintained through cutting.

A convincing document can still be fabricated. Certificate fraud exists in the steel trade, and visual inspection of a PDF will not reliably detect it. For high-consequence work, cross-check the heat number and key values with the issuing mill, or specify 3.2 with witnessed sampling.

Frequently asked questions

Is type 3.1 a third-party certificate?

No. Type 3.1 is issued and validated internally by the manufacturer, by a representative who must be independent of the production department. Externally witnessed validation is type 3.2.

Can I request 3.1 for aluminium?

Yes. EN 10204 is not steel-specific. For aluminium extrusions you should see the alloy designation and temper — EN AW-6063 in T5 or T6, for instance — along with composition and mechanical properties.

What if a supplier offers type 2.2 instead?

For non-critical components that is normal and reasonable. For load-bearing members where you asked for 3.1, it is a material change to the offer and should be treated as such. Ask why, and ask what it costs to upgrade.

Does the certificate expire?

The certificate describes a batch and does not expire. What does lapse is relevance — if material has been stored long enough that its condition is in question, or if the certificate cannot be traced to the physical item, the paperwork has lost practical value regardless of its date.

Should I ask for 3.1 on everything?

No. Specifying it across an entire bill of materials including decorative trim inflates cost without improving the structure. Reserve it for load-bearing and consequential members, and say so in the enquiry.
