How Many Units Fit in a 20'GP? Loading Estimates for Light Boxes and Bus Shelters

2026-11-05 · 10 min read · Engineering Team, ZhanXin

When a buyer asks how many units ship in one container, they are rarely asking about curiosity. They are doing arithmetic on landed cost — and they are right to, because for products in this category freight is a large share of what actually arrives at site.

Our minimum order quantity is one 20'GP container, which makes this question the natural next one. This article explains how the numbers are derived, why these particular products almost always run out of space rather than weight, and which packing decisions move the answer most.

A caveat up front, and an important one: every figure below is illustrative, and — critically — units per container cannot be stated without knowing the unit dimensions. The same container holds several hundred compact signs or a single large shelter. Treat the numbers here as a method for estimating your own count, then ask for a packing plan for your specific order rather than relying on a rule of thumb.

Start from the container itself

These are the commonly published internal dimensions. Individual containers vary slightly, and shipping lines publish their own figures.

Note the door opening on a 20'GP is typically around 2,340 mm wide by 2,280 mm high — narrower and lower than the interior. Anything rigid and near full width has to pass that opening, and this single fact constrains a surprising number of packing plans.

The decisive fact: this is bulky cargo, not heavy cargo

A light box or a shelter frame is mostly air. A 20'GP will carry around 28 tonnes, but it only holds 33 cubic metres.

That mismatch explains everything about how these shipments behave:

You will fill the container's volume while it is still carrying well under its weight limit.

If this were steel plate or machinery, the opposite would be true — weight would be the binding constraint and volume the slack. For our products, volume is the scarce resource, which leads to a straightforward conclusion:

Every cubic metre you can remove from each unit is worth roughly the same as cash, because it lets another unit into the same freight bill.

This is why knock-down packing dominates in this industry, and why it should be settled during design rather than discovered at the loading bay.

Knock-down versus assembled

Consider a mid-size shelter, say around 6 m long by 1.5 m deep by 2.8 m high.

ApproachRough volumetric behaviourTypical outcome
Shipped assembledEach unit occupies its full external envelope; units cannot be stacked or nestedVery few units per container. Freight per unit is high
Shipped knocked down (KD)Roof panels, glazing, columns and beams flat-pack and nest in shared cratesMaterially more units per container

The precise multiple depends heavily on how well parts nest, but it is common for KD packing to change the unit freight cost by a large factor, not a rounding error. If you are comparing two quotes with different packing assumptions, you are not comparing like with like — check this before concluding that one supplier is expensive.

The trade-off is real, though, and it belongs to your budget, not the supplier's: KD shipping shifts labour from our factory to your site. You will need assembly time, a crew, lifting equipment and clear instructions. Pre-assembled units reduce site work and skill requirements but cost more to ship. Decide based on your local labour cost and the availability of skilled installation crews.

Loading quantity is a function of unit size — there is no single number

"How many units fit in a 20'GP?" has no fixed answer, and this is the single most important thing to understand before comparing freight quotations. A compact hanging sign and a full bus shelter differ by two orders of magnitude in packed volume, so any figure quoted without the unit size attached to it is meaningless.

What you can do reliably is convert your unit dimensions into a packed volume, then divide it into the container.

The calculation

Units per container ≈ usable container volume ÷ packed volume per unit × loading efficiency

Same product, same order, roughly a 2.5× swing in what one container carries.

Example 2 — when the unit is larger than the box

A pillar-wrapped light box may be specified up to around 3 m wide by 5 m high. The interior height of a 20'GP is about 2,393 mm and the door opening lower still. Anything taller than that cannot stand upright in the container no matter how much volume is free. It has to travel as a horizontal split (upper and lower sections joined on site) or flat-packed. This is purely a function of unit size, and it is why tall units are quoted as KD by default.

Example 3 — when the unit is longer than the box

Hanging LED structures can be specified from 3 m up to 12 m in length. Anything approaching or exceeding 5,898 mm leaves no margin at all along a 20'GP. Such units are produced as bolted sections, which restores margin and usually improves the count — but it adds a splice detail that has to be engineered and sealed, not improvised.

Six factors that change the count

1. Unit external dimensions. The dominant variable. Packed volume scales with the panel area of a light box or the envelope of a shelter, and the unit count moves in inverse proportion. Two buyers quoting "one container" can be discussing forty units or four.

2. Nesting geometry. Parts designed to stack inside one another beat parts that merely sit side by side. This is a design decision made weeks before shipping.

3. The longest single part. A shelter may flat-pack beautifully, but if it has a rigid 5.8 m roof beam, that beam dictates orientation along the container's length. Anything approaching 5,898 mm leaves essentially no margin and can create loading problems.

4. Packaging specification. Cartons on pallets, wooden crates, and returnable steel stillage each consume different amounts of space. We will recommend one based on your product and route.

5. Protection level. Marine-grade packing means edge protection, vapour barriers and blocking. Under-specify and you save a little volume while risking salt-air corrosion and impact damage on a six-week voyage.

6. Door opening limits. Already noted, but it bears repeating — a rigid item slightly wider than about 2,340 mm will not enter through the doors of a standard container no matter how much interior space remains.

The three items that catch buyers out

These are not about volume, but they are the most common causes of unexpected cost and delay.

Lithium batteries change the shipping class

If your shelters include solar storage, backup batteries, or any rechargeable cell, the consignment may become dangerous goods. Lithium batteries ship under UN3480 (batteries alone) or UN3481 (batteries packed with or contained in equipment).

That typically means dangerous goods booking, a packing declaration, test summary documentation, and possible surcharges or stowage restrictions. It does not make the project unworkable, but it changes both cost and schedule, so it has to be declared during quotation.

This is one reason we ask about power configuration early in our shelter configuration checklist rather than at order confirmation.

Solid wood packing needs ISPM 15

Any solid wood packaging material — crates, pallets, dunnage — normally requires treatment to ISPM 15, evidenced by the stamped mark. Untreated wood is a frequent cause of quarantine rejection and forced re-packing at destination, which is expensive and slow. Engineered wood products such as plywood are generally treated differently, but you should confirm rather than assume.

Confirm your packing specification with your freight forwarder before production, not after.

Demurrage and detention are calendar costs

Container free time is limited. Once it expires, storage charges accrue daily. It sounds obvious, but container dwell caused by documentation errors or site unreadiness is one of the largest avoidable costs in this kind of project.

Frequently asked questions

How many units fit in a 20'GP?

It depends primarily on the external size of each unit and secondarily on how it is packed. Convert your unit dimensions into a packed volume and divide it into roughly 28 m³ of usable space. Across our product range the answer spans from a hundred-plus compact signs down to one fully assembled shelter, so always state unit size when you ask.

Why is the minimum order one container?

Because less-than-container-load shipping on bulky goods tends to cost disproportionately more per unit and carries higher damage risk.

Is knocked-down always better?

Usually for freight, not always overall. It moves assembly labour to your site. Compare against your local labour cost and crew capability.

Can I mix products in one container?

Yes, and it often improves unit economics. Ask us to combine related street furniture into one shipment.

Does weight ever matter?

Rarely for these products, which is why the useful question is always "how many cubic metres," not "how many kilos."

What happens if my shelter has solar panels and batteries?

Declare it early. Batteries may bring the consignment into dangerous goods rules, affecting cost, booking and transit time.
