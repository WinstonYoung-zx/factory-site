### 0.1 为什么媒体和博主会引用它

这份报告的价值**不在于"我们公司很厉害"，而在于"我们把散落在各处的合规知识整理成了一张可查的表"**。记者写采购指南、博主写选型科普、招标顾问做培训材料时，最缺的是：

1. **可核对的硬信息**——法规号、标准号、分级阈值、IP 等级测试条件。这些东西他们不敢凭记忆写，必须找来源。本报告把 CE / CPR / EN 1090 / SASO / SABER / ECAS / G-Mark / IEC 60529 / EN 10204 / WPS-PQR 等要点集中在一处，且每条都标了可核查的标准编号。
2. **能直接摘录的表格**——Appendix 的"合规速查表"、各章的对比表（LED vs LCD、IP 等级、EXC1–EXC4、EN 10204 类型）都是为"被截图、被引用"设计的。
3. **诚实的定位反而加分**——报告扉页明确写"compiled from our technical guides and hands-on project experience; not a market survey"。没有假数据（如"60% 的采购商认为……"），就不会被拆穿；"基于经验"的定性判断单独标注，可信度更高。

> **重要**：分发时对外只说"a practical compliance & selection reference compiled from our technical guides"，**不要**把它包装成"original market research / industry survey data"——我们没有调研数据，虚报会被行业人士一眼识破，反而毁掉链接诱饵的长期价值。

### Executive Summary

"Do you have CE?" "Send us your CE certificate." "Is it IP67?" These are among the most frequently exchanged — and least useful — sentences in outdoor procurement. Each collapses a specific, product-dependent question into a yes-or-no answer, and each predicts a conversation that will go badly at customs.

This guide exists to replace those shorthand questions with the right ones. It is written for procurement teams, specifiers, tender writers and project managers who buy street furniture, wayfinding signage, bus shelters, light boxes and outdoor display structures — particularly those buying from outside the EU or the Gulf and supplying into those markets.

**What this guide does:**

- Separates the four things buyers most often conflate: a **declaration** (CE), a **standard** (EN 1090), a **national programme** (ECAS / SASO) and a **mark** (G-Mark).
- Gives the real decision criteria behind technical specifications — the ~98-inch boundary between LCD and LED, why high-brightness LCD is rated at 2,500–3,500 nits while sun-exposed LED runs 5,500 nits and above, and why IP66 matters more to street-level equipment than IP67.
- Turns documentation into a checklist you can paste into an RFQ: EN 10204 3.1, WPS/PQR/WPQ, FPC certification, DoP, IP and salt-spray test reports.
- Names the compliance mistakes that cost schedules — ISO 9001 mistaken for CE, a test report mistaken for a conformity file, a shipment certificate forgotten in Saudi Arabia.

**How to use it.** Read Chapter 1 and 2 for the regulatory map of your destination market. Use Chapter 3 when you are choosing a display or enclosure. Use Chapter 4 as a tender checklist. Keep the Appendix one-page table next to your RFQ.

**A note on honesty.** The single most valuable thing a supplier can do in the first email is state precisely what it can and cannot provide, and which obligations sit with the buyer. Much of the language in this guide reflects that principle: we would rather you verify an evidence package than accept a broad claim. Regulation (EU) 2024/3110 replaced CPR 305/2011 and applies from January 2026 — confirm current texts before finalising procurement documents.

### Chapter 1 — EU Market: CE Marking & CPR

#### 1.1 The first correction: CE is a declaration, not a certificate

CE is not a certificate issued to a factory. It is a **declaration the manufacturer makes**, backed by a documented process. A factory does not "hold CE" — what it holds is the evidence supporting a specific declaration, for a specific product, against a specific legal requirement, signed by a specific party.

The honest answer to "do you have CE?" is always another question: **for which product, under which instrument, declared by whom, for which market?**

This distinction determines everything downstream: who signs, who is liable, what the evidence file must contain, and how long it takes before anything ships.

#### 1.2 Three routes, not one

The applicable legal instrument depends on what the product actually is. For street furniture and display work, three families matter:

| Product family | Typical EU legal basis | What it drives |
|---|---|---|
| Load-bearing steel or aluminium structural components placed permanently in works | Construction Products Regulation (EU) 305/2011 — now replaced by Regulation (EU) 2024/3110, applicable from January 2026 — with EN 1090-1 as harmonised standard | Factory Production Control (FPC) certification by a notified body, Declaration of Performance (DoP), CE label |
| Non-structural units not covered by a harmonised construction standard — many freestanding street furniture items | Often outside CPR; then General Product Safety Regulation (EU) 2023/988 plus national requirements | Safety documentation, instructions in the destination language, traceability |
| Illuminated units, screens and powered equipment | LVD 2014/35/EU, EMC 2014/30/EU, RoHS 2011/65/EU; ErP requirements for light sources; RED if radio is included | Technical file, test evidence, EU declaration of conformity |

An illuminated light box is therefore not certified under the construction route simply because it stands on a pavement. Confirm the instrument per product.

#### 1.3 Who is legally on the hook

Under the CPR and the product directives, obligations attach to defined economic operators: the **manufacturer**, an **authorised representative** established in the Union, the **importer**, and the **distributor**. A manufacturer established outside the EU generally cannot discharge CE obligations alone — someone established in the Union carries part of the duty, and by default that is the importer.

| Arrangement | Reality |
|---|---|
| Non-EU factory sells to an EU buyer who resells under their own name | The EU buyer may be treated as importer and often takes on manufacturer-level obligations |
| Non-EU factory appoints an EU authorised representative | Representative holds specific duties under mandate; factory retains product responsibility |
| Unit designed and built for one identified works, by a party also responsible for installation | A narrower CPR route may exist for custom-made non-series products — confirm with the responsible designer, never assume |

Ask your supplier two questions: what exactly can they provide, and what exactly do they expect **you** to provide. Vague answers here predict problems at customs.

#### 1.4 What the evidence file contains

For structural steel and aluminium under EN 1090-1, expect the following to be requested:

| Element | Notes |
|---|---|
| Declaration of Performance (DoP) | Declares essential characteristics in EN 1090-1: tolerance class, execution class achieved, welding-related performance, durability method |
| Factory Production Control documentation | The system describing how quality is maintained — overlapping with, but not the same as, ISO 9001 |
| FPC certification by a notified body | Mandatory under EN 1090-1 for most cases; assessed at the factory |
| Welding coordination and qualification | Coordination per ISO 14731; WPS qualified by PQR; welders qualified per ISO 9606 |
| Material traceability | EN 10204 certificates plus marking transfer through cutting and fabrication |
| Durability evidence | Coating specification and thickness records, salt spray per ISO 9227, or declared alternative protection |
| The CE label itself | Prescribed content, on product or accompanying documentation, in the destination market's language |

The level of documentation and inspection follows the **declared execution class**. Declaring a higher class than your process supports is worse than declaring honestly — the audit, and any future claim, will find the gap.

#### 1.5 Realistic timelines

None of the following steps is a formality:

| Step | Rough reality |
|---|---|
| Gap analysis against the applicable instrument | Depends on how much documented process already exists |
| Building or upgrading FPC documentation | Usually the longest internal task — not the audit |
| Qualifying procedures and personnel (WPS/PQR, welder qualification) | Some steps involve testing that cannot be compressed |
| Notified body assessment and certification | Involves scheduling external auditors |
| Project-specific declaration and labelling | Fast once the system exists |

Two honest conclusions: if a route is not yet in place, treat it as a programme item to start **months** before shipment, not a clause to add to the purchase order. And where the requirement arises infrequently, the route most projects actually use is a **project-specific route with testing by a laboratory nominated by the buyer**, rather than maintaining certification that sits idle between orders.

#### 1.6 Six mistakes that cost schedules

1. **Assuming ISO 9001 equals CE.** It does not. ISO 9001 certifies a quality management system; no notified body accepts it as a substitute for product-specific assessment.
2. **Accepting a test report as proof of conformity.** A report states what was measured on the samples submitted. It is evidence *within* the file, not the file.
3. **Using stale declared performance.** Change your coating, material source or welding process and you have arguably changed the basis of the declaration. Record change, then re-assess.
4. **Confusing the two names on the documents.** A test report naming one entity and a declaration signed by another raises questions immediately. Keep the chain coherent: who made it, who tested it, who declared it, who imported it.
5. **Ignoring language requirements.** Instructions and safety information in the destination language are not optional extras.
6. **Declaring everything "NPD"** (no performance determined). Legitimate for some characteristics, but declaring everything NPD reads as an incomplete file, and some contracting authorities reject it outright.

#### 1.7 EN 1090 execution classes — what buyers outside the EU need to know

EN 1090 is the harmonised European standard for execution (fabrication and erection) of steel and aluminium structures:

| Part | Scope |
|---|---|
| EN 1090-1 | Requirements for conformity assessment of structural components — the part governing CE marking |
| EN 1090-2 | Technical requirements for steel structures |
| EN 1090-3 | Technical requirements for aluminium structures |

EN 1090 defines **four execution classes**, EXC1 to EXC4. The class sets the level of quality control across welding supervision, material traceability, tolerances, surface preparation, inspection and documentation. Higher class = more control = more documentation = higher cost. It does **not** mean "better built" in a general sense: a component correctly made to EXC2 is not inferior to one made to EXC3; it serves a structure with lower consequences of failure.

| Class | Typical application | What it implies |
|---|---|---|
| EXC1 | Non-critical, lightly loaded agricultural or storage structures; some secondary components | Basic control; light tolerances and documentation; welding coordination generally not mandatory |
| EXC2 | Default for ordinary buildings and most architectural metalwork — frames for shelters, canopies, signage, display units | Documented welding coordination, traceable materials, defined inspection regime — where most commercial work sits |
| EXC3 | Failure would have serious consequences: bridges, grandstands, high-occupancy public buildings, masts and towers | Comprehensive welding coordination, stricter tolerances, expanded NDT, formally qualified personnel |
| EXC4 | Extreme consequence or special service — long-span bridges, offshore, certain nuclear/special applications | Additional requirements agreed case by case on top of EXC3 |

**Who decides the class?** The project's **responsible designer**, not the fabricator and not the buyer. EN 1090 sets it from three inputs: **consequence class** (CC1–CC3, per EN 1990), **service category** (SR1 essentially static; SR2 fatigue-relevant — traffic, crowd vibration, wind cycling), and **production category** (PC2 shop specialising in metalworking; PC1 where metalwork is incidental).

| Consequence class | Service category | Typical resulting class |
|---|---|---|
| CC1 | SR1 | EXC1 |
| CC2 | SR1 | EXC2 |
| CC2 | SR2 | EXC2 to EXC3 |
| CC3 | SR1 or SR2 | EXC3 to EXC4 |

Use the tables in EN 1090-2 / -3 and the designer's specification — never a simplified chart alone. Three consequences: if the specification states no class, ask the designer to state one; a supplier quoting "EN 1090 certified" without naming the class has told you very little; raising the class "to seem cautious" will be quoted for.

**Is EN 1090 even applicable?** It covers structural (load-bearing) components. Purely non-structural architectural elements often fall outside it and are governed instead by product-specific requirements. In street furniture: bus shelter and canopy frames are normally in scope (typically EXC2); a ground-standing display frame carrying wind load is structural, a wall-recessed unit fixed to a building usually is not; tall ground-mounted signage is usually structural, small wall-mounted signs usually are not.

**Document checklist to put in your RFQ:**

1. **FPC certificate** from an EU notified body — check four things: standard (EN 1090-1), execution class(es), product scope, expiry. A certificate without class and scope is close to worthless.
2. **Declaration of Performance** — referenced to essential characteristics.
3. **Welding coordination evidence** — per EN ISO 14731; commonly specific level for EXC2, comprehensive level for EXC3/EXC4.
4. **WPS and PQR plus welder qualifications** — the WPS shows the joint design was qualified, not assumed; welder certificates must be current and matched to process and position.
5. **Material certificates to EN 10204** — in practice 3.1, validated by the manufacturer's authorised inspection representative independent of production.
6. **Traceability records** — heat/cast number from raw material to finished component; the single best indicator that a workshop really operates a control system.
7. **Inspection and test records** — dimensional checks, coating thickness, salt spray or other durability results.

Many Middle East and international tenders reference EN 1090 as a proxy for fabrication quality even where CE marking is not legally required. In those cases the documentation matters more than the mark.

### Chapter 2 — Gulf States: SASO / SABER / ECAS / G-Mark

Gulf projects are among the most paperwork-sensitive: the physical product is rarely the problem — what holds containers at port is almost always a certificate that should have been arranged before the vessel sailed.

#### 2.1 The four names, sorted

| Name | What it actually is | Where it applies |
|---|---|---|
| **SASO** | Saudi Standards, Metrology and Quality Organization — the standards body issuing Saudi technical regulations | Saudi Arabia |
| **SABER** | The Saudi online platform through which conformity certificates are applied for and issued | Saudi Arabia |
| **ECAS** | Emirates Conformity Assessment Scheme — the UAE conformity programme operated by the ministry responsible for industry | United Arab Emirates |
| **G-Mark** | The Gulf conformity mark, administered within the GCC framework through GSO (GCC Standardization Organization) | Across participating Gulf states, for regulated product categories |

SASO is an **organisation**, SABER is its **platform**, ECAS is a **national programme**, G-Mark is a **mark**. Asking "do you have G-Mark?" mixes a category of question with a category of answer — which is why nobody gives a straight reply.

#### 2.2 Start with the HS code, not the regulation list

The single most useful first step is mechanical: establish the **HS (customs) code** for each line item, then check whether that code is on the destination's regulated list. If it is not regulated, none of these schemes apply and the conversation ends there. If it is, the code tells you which route applies.

| Product family | Typical driver in the Gulf |
|---|---|
| Structural steel or aluminium assemblies | Often outside electrical regimes; governed by municipal/project specifications, authority approvals and structural requirements |
| Illuminated units and light boxes | Electrical safety and — importantly — energy-efficiency labelling once light sources are present |
| LED displays and powered equipment | Electrical safety and EMC; check whether the category falls under the Gulf low-voltage regime |
| Anything with radio modules or wireless control | Additional equipment-approval considerations, separate from conformity marking |

Where equipment registration applies under the Gulf low-voltage technical regulation, a G-Mark route with a conformity certificate from an approved body **may** be required. That phrase is deliberate: the regulated product list and its scope change — check it, do not memorise it.

#### 2.3 The distinction that matters most: product vs consignment

European practice trains buyers to think of certification as a property of the factory. Gulf practice is largely **shipment-bound** — the source of most schedule failures.

Saudi Arabia (SABER) typically involves two stages:

| Stage | Applies to | Roughly speaking |
|---|---|---|
| Product Certificate of Conformity | The product type | Issued for a defined period after review of technical documentation and test reports |
| Shipment Certificate of Conformity | Each consignment | Applied for per shipment, referencing invoice, packing list and transport documents |

You can hold valid product documentation and still be unable to clear a shipment because nobody applied for the shipment certificate before arrival. It is administrative, not technical — which is exactly why it is easy to overlook and expensive to discover. UAE (ECAS) follows similar logic, often requiring a **locally registered importer** as applicant. Make both stages explicit line items in the programme with named owners; if nobody owns the consignment certificate date, assume it will be late.

#### 2.4 Who can apply: the importer question

Most Gulf conformity routes require an applicant established in the destination market — generally your importer or their authorised representative.

- Decide who the applicant is **before production**. Their details appear on documentation; changing them mid-project is re-submission, not a name swap.
- Keep details consistent everywhere. Commercial invoice, packing list, certificate application, transport documents and product labelling must all agree on importer name, country of origin, item descriptions and quantities. Inconsistency is the most common reason an otherwise compliant shipment is held.

#### 2.5 Documentation you will be asked for

Expect a coherent pack rather than a single document:

| Item | Why it is requested |
|---|---|
| Test reports from an accredited or approved laboratory | Evidence for the technical regulation concerned |
| Product technical file with drawings and bills of materials | Demonstrates that what was tested matches what ships |
| Manufacturing quality documentation | Evidence of consistency (see Chapter 1 evidence file) |
| Material evidence | EN 10204 certificates and traceability records |
| Photographs and identification marks of shipped units | Lets inspectors match documents to goods |
| Arabic labelling and instructions | A substantive requirement, not a translation afterthought |
| Invoice, packing list, bill of lading, certificate of origin | Referenced by the consignment certificate |

A practical detail that repeatedly causes trouble: **product identification marks**. If units carry no serial or marking scheme, inspectors cannot match paperwork to goods and a physical inspection may be triggered. Decide the marking format during drawing. Arabic labelling should be assumed durable and integral to the product, not applied on arrival.

#### 2.6 Where the cost actually lands

Rarely the fee — the calendar costs:

- **Certificate timing vs vessel schedule.** A consignment certificate arriving after the ship costs days, not dollars.
- **Re-testing after a design change.** A LED driver substitution, a different light source or a coating change can invalidate the basis of a test report. Sequence a change control: notify before substitutions happen.
- **Demurrage.** Container free time is short; delay compounds daily.
- **Re-labelling at destination.** Correcting labels after arrival is the most expensive way to fix a translation issue.

Plan conformity steps backwards from the vessel date.

#### 2.7 Heat is a design input, not a finishing touch

Gulf ambient conditions involve high solar load, airborne dust and salt in coastal cities:

| Consideration | Why it is different here |
|---|---|
| Brightness and automatic dimming | High ambient light year-round; night dimming still required |
| Sealing against dust | IP must be considered together with drainage and filter maintenance |
| Corrosion protection | Coastal exposure raises demanded coating performance; ISO 9227 salt spray evidence becomes commercially relevant |
| Thermal management | Enclosures must reject heat while facing the sun |

Municipal and developer approvals frequently exceed product conformity; structural packages often involve execution-class considerations of the EN 1090 kind even where CE is not legally required — the engineering discipline transfers.

### Chapter 3 — Technical Selection: LED vs LCD, IP Ratings

#### 3.1 LED or LCD — the decision map

"We need a screen for the concourse" is not yet a specification. The two technologies fail in different ways, for different reasons, and those differences map onto site conditions.

| Site condition | Usually the better choice | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Single-panel up to around 98 inches, indoor or sheltered | LCD | Lower cost, finer image at close range, replace the unit |
| Anything larger than about 100 inches | LED | LCD panels stop around that size; above it you tile and pay for visible bezels |
| Direct sun on the screen face | LED | Much higher output, no panel blackening from heat |
| Close reading distance (under about 3 m) | LCD | Fine text needs resolution per m² that is costly in LED |
| Long-distance viewing (over 8–10 m) | LED | Fine detail is wasted; size and brightness carry the message |
| Curved, column-wrapped or irregular shapes | LED | Modularity follows the frame |
| Tightest capital budget, sheltered location | LCD | Cheaper per installed unit at small sizes |

**Size is a physical boundary.** LCD panels are manufactured in fixed sizes; the practical ceiling for a single reliable commercial panel is around **98 inches** — roughly 2.2 m diagonal, about 1.2 m × 2.2 m. Beyond that you are buying a video wall: multiple panels, a controller, calibration and bezels (visible seams of a few millimetres even on narrow-bezel units). LED has no such boundary — modular cabinets make size a design choice.

**Sunlight: brightness plus a failure mode buyers do not expect.** High-brightness LCD panels typically deliver around **2,500–3,500 nits** — plenty under a canopy, marginal in full afternoon sun. LED modules for genuinely sun-exposed positions routinely run **5,500 nits and above**. The harder problem is thermal: an LCD's liquid-crystal layer has a clearing temperature; direct sun on a dark enclosure can heat the surface enough to cause *isotropic blackening* (sun blemish) — irregular dark patches that may or may not recover, a specification issue rather than a manufacturing defect. Mitigations (air-gapped AR glass, active cooling, sun shields, orientation) add cost and depth.

**Viewing distance and content.** A working rule: minimum comfortable viewing distance in metres is roughly the pixel pitch in millimetres. For dense text at 2–3 m, LCD readability per unit cost is hard to beat; for brand video at 10 m plus, LED size and luminosity carry it.

**Service life and spares.** LCD backlights commonly reach around **50,000 hours to half brightness**, shortened by heat; panels are replaced as units, with a hidden risk of model obsolescence on roughly annual cycles — buy one spare with the project. LED modules are individually replaceable and commonly quote around **100,000 hours**, a figure meaningful only when tied to a brightness-retention point and operating temperature; buy spare modules and one spare driver per cabinet type in the first shipment.

**Running cost.** Ask for typical consumption at your specified brightness with representative content, plus the figure after automatic night dimming — not a single wattage. Compare per square metre of display area, and compare alternatives on the same basis.

**Public-space protection.** The mechanical-impact rating is **IK**, defined in IEC 62262; specify it alongside IP — neither implies the other. IK08 for normal public areas, IK10 where vandalism is expected; laminated or tempered glazing with AR treatment where reflections compete with content; lockable enclosure, concealed fasteners, no external cable access.

#### 3.2 Reading the IP code

IEC 60529 defines the Ingress Protection code as two numerals with optional letters. An **"X" means that characteristic was not tested** — IPX7 tells you nothing about dust protection.

**First numeral — solids and dust:**

| Numeral | Protected against |
|---|---|
| 0 | No protection |
| 1 | Objects 50 mm and larger |
| 2 | Objects 12.5 mm and larger |
| 3 | Objects 2.5 mm and larger |
| 4 | Objects 1.0 mm and larger |
| 5 | Dust protected — ingress not fully prevented, no harmful deposit permitted |
| 6 | Dust tight — no ingress of dust permitted |

For outdoor electronics ask for **6**, not 5: fine dust plus humidity forms a conductive film on driver boards.

**Second numeral — water:**

| Numeral | Protected against | Typical test condition |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | Water jets | 6.3 mm nozzle, approx. 12.5 L/min, 3 m distance |
| 6 | Powerful water jets | 12.5 mm nozzle, approx. 100 L/min, 3 m distance |
| 7 | Temporary immersion | 30 minutes at 1 m depth |
| 8 | Continuous immersion | Depth/duration set by manufacturer |
| 9 | High-pressure, high-temperature jets | Close range, ~80 °C |

IP6X takes roughly **eight times the water flow** of IP5X. IP7 involves still water at a fixed depth — no jet at all.

**Why IP67 does not replace IP66.** The second numeral does not cascade downward. An enclosure tested for temporary immersion has demonstrated nothing about driving rain, pressure washing or storm-driven water against a façade — it is entirely possible to pass IP67 and fail IP66.

| Stated rating | What you actually know | What you do not know |
|---|---|---|
| IP65 | Withstands water jets | Powerful jets or immersion |
| IP67 | Survives 30 min at 1 m depth | Jets; dust if written IPX7 |
| IP65 + IP67 | Both conditions tested | Powerful jets (IP66) |
| IP66 + IP67 | Powerful jets and immersion | IK impact, salt spray, UV aging |

For street-level installations **IP66** is usually the rating that matters most — road-side, platform-edge and coastal-promenade equipment sees wind-driven rain and regular pressure washing. A practical specification often reads **IP66 + IP67**.

**Condensation — the failure IP does not cover.** Outdoor enclosures heat in the sun and cool after dark; warm air holds moisture that condenses on diffusers, LED boards and drivers. An enclosure can be perfectly sealed to IP67 and still fill with internally generated water, because IP tests water trying to get *in*, not water already inside trying to get *out*. Responses, in order of preference: **breathable vent plugs** (hydrophobic membrane — the standard answer, specify by default for LED units); **drainage provisions** (low-point weep path, maintenance-dependent); **active dehumidification** (justified only for high-value monitored equipment). Note the conflict: a drainage hole and a higher IP rating work against each other.

**Four things IP ratings do not tell you:**

| Requirement | Standard to reference | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Impact resistance | IEC 62262 / EN 62262 (IK07–IK10) | Public furniture is struck by carts, luggage, cleaning equipment; specify IK08+ at street level |
| Salt spray / corrosion | ISO 9227 or ASTM B117 | Coastal and de-iced-road environments; ask for tested hours and substrate |
| Organic coating durability | Qualicoat / GSB / AAMA (Al), ISO 12944 (steel) | Chalking and fading are the most common visible failures |
| Fire performance | EN 13501-1 (EU) or UL 94 | Mandatory indoor/semi-indoor; relevant to diffuser and panel materials |

A coastal, high-traffic platform installation therefore needs something closer to: **IP66 + IP67, IK08, 1,000 h neutral salt spray per ISO 9227, Qualicoat Class 2** — four independent requirements, only one of which is an IP rating.

**Matching rating to installation:**

| Installation condition | Recommended minimum | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Indoor, climate controlled | IP20–IP40 | Dust management only; higher spec traps heat |
| Semi-outdoor, under canopy | IP54 | Include condensation control |
| Standard outdoor façade / street furniture | IP65 | Where powerful jets will not occur |
| Exposed outdoor, street level, or regularly washed | IP66 | Realistic default for shelters, platform units, street-facing displays |
| Flood-prone or below grade | IP66 + IP67/IP68 | Both conditions separately tested and stated |

"IP69K" is not part of IEC 60529 — the K belongs to ISO 20653 (road vehicles). Do not use it for general building applications unless asked. An IP rating is demonstrated on a new unit in laboratory conditions: gaskets age, UV degrades polymers, fastener compression relaxes — ask about gasket material (EPDM vs silicone) and replacement interval.

### Chapter 4 — Documentation Checklist for Tender & Export

A tender document is not won by the broadest compliance claim; it is won by a coherent, checkable evidence pack. Four document families do most of the work.

#### 4.1 Material certificates — EN 10204

EN 10204 (*Metallic products — Types of inspection documents*) defines **document types, not material quality**.

| Type | Name | Who validates | Test results? | Specific to this batch? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | Declaration of compliance with order | Manufacturer | No | No |
| 2.2 | Test report | Manufacturer | Yes — non-specific inspection | No — routine testing |
| 3.1 | Inspection certificate 3.1 | Manufacturer's authorised inspection representative, independent of production | Yes — specific inspection | Yes — this batch |
| 3.2 | Inspection certificate 3.2 | Manufacturer's representative + purchaser's representative or third party | Yes — specific inspection | Yes — this batch |

**The expensive misunderstanding:** type 3.1 is **not a third-party certificate**. It is issued by the manufacturer; the requirement is internal separation of duties. Genuinely external witness is type 3.2. 3.1 is nevertheless the correct, standard, sufficient expectation for most load-bearing fabrication — specifying 3.2 when you meant 3.1 adds cost and lead time for no benefit.

| Situation | Sensible requirement |
|---|---|
| Non-load-bearing decorative metalwork | 2.2 often adequate |
| Load-bearing components in ordinary building work | 3.1 — standard expectation |
| EXC3/EXC4, pressure equipment, or contractually mandated | 3.2, witnessed or co-validated |

**How to read a 3.1 in one minute:** (1) does the heat/cast number appear and match the physical material? (2) is the designation complete to a named standard (e.g. S275JR to EN 10025; EN AW-6063 T5/T6 for aluminium)? (3) are chemical composition values present for your batch? (4) are mechanical properties present (yield, tensile, elongation)? (5) is it signed, dated and stamped by the authorised representative? (6) do dimensions, quantity and form match your order? A certificate describes the raw material, not your fabricated part — welding, forming and coating are covered by other documents.

#### 4.2 Welding documentation — WPS, PQR, WPQ

Welding is a special process: once a joint is finished and painted, its internal quality cannot be verified by looking at it. The industry therefore qualifies the method, qualifies the person, and supervises them.

| Document | What it is | Analogy |
|---|---|---|
| WPS — Welding Procedure Specification | Instruction sheet: method, filler, position, parameters, preheat — issued by the manufacturer | The recipe |
| PQR / WPQR — Procedure Qualification Record | Recorded evidence that following the recipe produced a joint that passed testing, from actual physical testing | The test bake |
| WPQ — Welder Performance Qualification | Proof that a named person can execute the procedure competently, after witnessed testing | The cook's certificate |

The relationship runs one way: **PQR comes first** — test the joint, record results, then write the production WPS. A WPS with no supporting PQR is a guess written down. Having all three documents proves *capability*; supervision under ISO 14731 proves *execution*.

**When it matters:** load-bearing members and connections subject to wind or crowd loading — qualified WPS with supporting PQR and current WPQ; EN 1090 EXC2 and above — plus documented welding coordination; fatigue-loaded details (vehicle impact, barriers) — additional attention to detail category and possibly NDT. Street furniture deserves thought: a shelter frame sees constant wind cycling over decades, and a marginally fused fillet at a base connection is exactly where a fatigue crack starts.

**Governing standards:** ISO 15607 (general rules), ISO 15614-1 (steel) / -2 (aluminium) procedure tests, ISO/TR 15608 (material grouping), ISO 9606-1/-2 (welder qualification), ISO 6947 (positions), ISO 14731 (coordination), ISO 17637 (visual testing).

**Reading a WPS:** check process number per ISO 4063 (111 MMA, 135 MAG, 141 TIG — aluminium is normally TIG/MIG); parent material group per ISO/TR 15608 (structural steel commonly group 1; aluminium 21–23, 6063 in group 23); **thickness range covered** (the most often quietly mismatched item); joint type; filler metal; shielding gas; electrical parameters; preheat/interpass; position (PA flat, PB horizontal-vertical, PC horizontal, PD, PE overhead, PF vertical upward).

**Validity — the point nearly everyone gets wrong:** a **PQR does not expire** — it is a historical fact, and ISO 15614 does not require re-testing because the calendar moved. **Welder qualifications do**: under ISO 9606 generally valid two years from test date, extendable in six-month increments with employer confirmation of continued work within the qualified range. Ask for the certificate **plus the latest extension confirmation** — the second sheet most suppliers leave out.

#### 4.3 The consolidated RFQ checklist

| # | Item | Standard / note |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Product classification & applicable instrument | CPR/GPSR/LVD/EMC/RoHS (EU); HS-code driven (Gulf) |
| 2 | CE route, signatory and product scope agreed in writing | DoP / EU DoC; importer or authorised rep identified |
| 3 | Execution class (if applicable) and FPC certificate scope | EN 1090-1; class, scope, expiry, standard |
| 4 | Declaration of Performance / Declaration of Conformity | With essential characteristics, no blanket NPD |
| 5 | Material certificates, batch-specific | EN 10204 3.1 (or 3.2 where required) + traceability |
| 6 | WPS + supporting PQR | Matched material group, thickness, process, position |
| 7 | Welder qualifications with current extensions | ISO 9606; six-month confirmations |
| 8 | Welding coordinator qualification | ISO 14731; level matched to EXC |
| 9 | Coating thickness + salt spray records | ISO 9227 hours and substrate stated |
| 10 | IP and IK test reports | IEC 60529 / IEC 62262 — each declared combination tested |
| 11 | Dimensional inspection report | Against agreed tolerances |
| 12 | Destination-language labelling and instructions | Arabic for Gulf; destination language in EU |
| 13 | Shipment documentation consistency | Invoice, packing list, B/L, COO, certificate applications agree |
| 14 | Shipment/consignment certificate owner & date | Gulf — named owner, backward-planned from vessel |
| 15 | Third-party pre-shipment inspection | Arrange before production, not after |

Two scope notes that prevent disputes: the supplier should state in writing which obligations sit with it and which with the buyer; and if installation is not supplied by the factory, say so in the bid documents — installation drawings, manuals and remote video support are the realistic deliverable for an overseas fabrication supply.

### Chapter 5 — Common Compliance Mistakes

Drawn from the recurring errors in our technical guides, these are the mistakes that most often turn a compliant product into a delayed shipment.

1. **Treating CE as a certificate a factory holds.** It is a declaration by a defined party against a defined instrument. Ask *which product, which instrument, who signs, which market.*
2. **Equating ISO 9001 with product conformity.** A QMS certificate is not a product assessment and no notified body accepts it as one.
3. **Treating a test report as the whole file.** A report covers submitted samples; conformity is the file that contains it.
4. **Over-specifying the execution class.** EXC3 "to be safe" on ordinary architectural metalwork adds cost and lead time without adding safety — EXC2 is where most commercial work sits.
5. **Letting the fabricator choose the class.** The responsible designer sets it from consequence class, service category and production category.
6. **Assuming 3.1 is third-party, or asking for 3.2 unnecessarily.** 3.1 is internal; 3.2 is externally witnessed. Know which you are buying.
7. **Letting a PQR be rejected for age, or accepting an expired welder card.** PQRs do not expire; welder qualifications need current six-month confirmations.
8. **Specifying IP67 instead of IP66 for street-level equipment.** The numerals do not cascade; immersion says nothing about driving rain or pressure washing.
9. **Chasing a bigger IP number while ignoring condensation.** IP tests water coming in, not moisture generated inside — specify vent plugs and drainage strategy.
10. **Treating IP as the whole durability spec.** IK impact, ISO 9227 salt spray, coating class and fire performance are separate requirements.
11. **Putting "IP65" in a specification without a thermal strategy for a sun-facing LCD.** High-brightness LCD plus AR glass, cooling and orientation still carries residual blackening risk — or specify LED.
12. **Forgetting the Saudi shipment certificate.** Valid product documentation does not clear a consignment; the per-shipment CoC must be applied for before arrival.
13. **Changing a driver, light source or coating after testing.** A substitution can invalidate the test basis — operate change control.
14. **Inconsistent applicant/importer details across documents.** Invoice, packing list, certificates, transport documents and labels must all agree.
15. **Treating Arabic labelling as a translation afterthought.** Assume durable, integral markings, planned at artwork stage.
16. **Declaring everything "NPD".** Reads as an incomplete technical file and can be rejected outright.

*Experience note (not survey data): based on our experience serving projects in Europe, the Middle East and the Americas, the delays that surprise buyers most are administrative rather than technical — a missing consignment certificate, an unowned application date, a label corrected at destination. The physical product rarely causes the hold.*

- 若要 A/B：用两个 URL + UTM 分流，比较"外链数量/质量"与"线索数量"，让数据决定，而不是凭感觉。
